Cast iron is one of the few things you can buy once. A skillet from 1940 still cooks, which is not
something you can say about any non-stick pan made this decade.

That also means buying badly is expensive in a different way. You do not replace a bad cast iron
pan. You just resent it for twenty years.

## Size: get the 10.25 inch

This is the decision people get wrong most often. The 12 inch looks like better value and is the
most common gift, but a 12 inch skillet weighs around eight pounds empty. Add a chicken and you
are lifting eleven pounds with one hand at shoulder height, over a hot burner.

The 10.25 inch is the workhorse. It handles four eggs, two large chicken breasts, a pound of
ground meat, or a skillet cornbread. It fits in a standard oven next to something else. The
[Lodge 10.25 inch skillet](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00006JSUA) is the default recommendation
here for a reason: it is inexpensive enough that a mistake costs you twenty dollars.

Get the 12 inch second, once you know you want it.

## Surface finish matters more than brand

There are two schools of cast iron.

**Rough cast**, which is what Lodge and most budget pans use. The interior has a visible sandy
texture from the casting mold. It is completely functional and seasons up fine, it just takes
longer to get slick because the seasoning has to fill in the texture first.

**Machined smooth**, which is what Field Company, Smithey and vintage Griswold pans use. The
interior is milled flat after casting. It becomes non-stick faster, releases eggs better, and
costs three to five times more.

The honest answer: rough cast is not worse at cooking. It is worse at the specific thing people
notice, which is eggs sliding around like a commercial. If you cook eggs in cast iron daily, the
smooth pan is worth it. If you are searing steaks and baking cornbread, it is not.

## What to ignore

**Pre-seasoning claims.** Every pan sold today is pre-seasoned and none of them arrive non-stick.
It is rust protection for the shipping container. Your seasoning is the one that matters.

**Weight as a quality signal.** Heavier is not better. It is thicker, which means slower to heat
and slower to respond. Vintage pans that people prize are notably lighter than modern Lodge.

**Enameled versus bare, framed as an upgrade.** Enameled cast iron is a different tool. It does
not season, cannot take the same abuse, and is for braising rather than searing. It is not a
better version of a skillet.

## Seasoning it properly, once

Skip the six-round oven marathon. It is mostly internet folklore.

1. Wash the new pan with hot water and soap, then dry it on the burner until it is completely dry.
2. Rub a thin layer of a neutral high smoke point oil over every surface, including the handle and the underside.
3. Wipe it back off. It should look almost dry. Excess oil is what creates a sticky, blotchy finish.
4. Bake it upside down at 450F for one hour with a tray underneath, then let it cool in the oven.

Then cook in it. Bacon, fried potatoes, anything with fat. The pan improves every time you use it,
which is the entire point.

## Care rules that actually matter

- Dry it over heat, not on a rack. Residual water is the only thing that will genuinely ruin it.
- Do not soak it. Ten minutes is fine, overnight is rust.
- Store it dry with a paper towel inside if your kitchen is humid.
- If it does rust, scrub it back to bare metal and re-season. Cast iron is close to unkillable.

That is the whole maintenance regime. Everything else you read is preference dressed up as rule.
