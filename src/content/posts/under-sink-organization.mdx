The cabinet under your kitchen sink is usually the biggest wasted space in the room. It is a
deep, tall box, and most people use maybe a third of it because a drain pipe runs straight
through the middle and nothing rectangular fits around it.

So it becomes a graveyard. Half-empty spray bottles at the front, something sticky at the back
you have not identified since spring.

The fix is not buying more bins. It is measuring the space properly, deciding what actually
belongs there, and then choosing a shape that works around your plumbing instead of against it.
Done once, it roughly doubles what fits and you stop kneeling on the floor moving bottles to find
the dish soap.

## Measure before you buy anything

This is the step that decides whether your purchase works or goes back in the box. It takes two
minutes and you need three numbers.

| Measurement | How to take it | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Cabinet width and depth** | Inside edge to inside edge, not the door frame | The door opening is often narrower than the cabinet. An organizer has to fit through it |
| **Floor to lowest pipe** | From the cabinet base to the underside of the P-trap | This is your real height limit, not the cabinet height |
| **Back wall to front of P-trap** | Horizontally, at the pipe's widest point | Decides whether anything can sit behind the pipes |

That third measurement is the one nearly everyone skips, and it is the one that causes returns.
A two-tier organizer advertised as fitting a 24 inch cabinet will still collide with your drain
if the P-trap sits eight inches off the back wall.

Write the numbers on your phone before you shop. Product listings give exterior dimensions, so
you are comparing directly.

### The garbage disposal problem

If you have a disposal unit, your usable height on that side drops sharply, often to eight inches
or less. Treat the two sides of the cabinet as separate spaces with different height limits
rather than looking for one unit to span both.

## Decide what belongs there first

Organizing before you edit just gives you tidier clutter. Under-sink space is humid, occasionally
wet, and shares air with cleaning chemicals. That rules a lot out.

**Keep under the sink:**

- Dish soap, sponges and scrubbers you use daily
- Surface cleaners and glass cleaner
- Dishwasher tabs or powder
- Trash and recycling bags
- Rubber gloves
- A small caddy of cleaning supplies you can lift out whole

**Move somewhere else:**

- Anything edible, including pet food and paper-wrapped goods
- Paper towels and napkins, which absorb moisture and smell
- Small appliances and anything with a cord
- Backup supplies you buy in bulk, which belong in a pantry or garage
- Vases, which everyone stores here and nobody uses from here

If you have children or pets, this is also the moment to add a cabinet latch. Cleaning chemicals
at floor level is the single most common household poisoning setup.

## Zone the space in five parts

Once the cabinet is empty and measured, think of it as five zones rather than one hole.

1. **Under the pipes, front.** The most reachable spot. Daily items go here: dish soap, sponges, the cleaning caddy.
2. **Under the pipes, back.** Low and awkward. Use it for flat things you rarely touch, like spare sponges or a folded liner.
3. **Left or right of the plumbing, floor level.** Usually the largest clear rectangle. This is where a tiered organizer earns its place.
4. **Vertical, on the door.** Almost always unused. A door-mounted rack holds gloves, bags or small bottles without eating floor space.
5. **Above the pipes.** Only usable if your P-trap sits low. If you have six inches of clearance above it, a slim shelf doubles your capacity.

Most people only ever use zone 1 and part of zone 3. Zones 4 and 5 are free storage that costs
nothing but a tape measure.

## The upgrade nobody thinks of

Before you install anything, put down a waterproof cabinet liner mat.

Under-sink leaks are slow and quiet. By the time you notice a swollen particleboard base, the
repair is a new cabinet floor rather than a tightened fitting. A liner catches the drip, and more
usefully, it shows you the drip. A dark ring on a mat is a leak you can fix on a Saturday.

It is the cheapest thing in this guide and the only one that prevents a repair bill.

## Keeping it that way

The system only survives if putting things back is easier than not.

- Keep the daily cleaning items in a lift-out caddy. One grab, no kneeling.
- Leave one tier deliberately empty. Every system needs somewhere for the new thing to go.
- Check the liner mat when you swap the trash bag. Two seconds, once a week.
- Do not stack. If you cannot see it, you will buy a second one.

## The bottom line

Measure your P-trap clearance before you buy anything. That one number decides which shape works,
and it is the reason most under-sink organizers get returned.

If your plumbing sits centered, go L-shaped. If it runs to one side, go tall and adjustable. Add
a liner mat either way.
